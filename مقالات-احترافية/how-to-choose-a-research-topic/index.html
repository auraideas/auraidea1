Start with a problem, not a fashionable title

A strong topic is not simply a popular keyword. It is a manageable problem that can be examined with accessible evidence, a clear population or context, and an appropriate method. Begin by writing three areas you genuinely care about, then identify a concrete phenomenon, decision, or gap within each one.

Test the idea against five questions

CriterionDiagnostic question
ClarityCould another reader understand the focus without a verbal explanation?
FeasibilityCan you access the data, participants, or literature within your timeline?
ScopeIs the population, setting, and period narrow enough to study well?
ValueWill the result add understanding, comparison, or a useful decision?
EthicsCan the study protect participants and handle data responsibly?

Turn the interest into a researchable question

Replace “artificial intelligence in education” with a question that names the group, setting, and object of inquiry: “How do final-year students evaluate the usefulness of AI tools for organizing references in graduation projects?” The second version can guide a literature review and an instrument; the first is only a field.

Use a short literature scan as a filter

Record what each relevant source studied, how it collected evidence, what it found, and what it left unresolved. A literature review is not a list of quotations. It is a map of claims and methods that helps you avoid accidental duplication and make a credible contribution.

Write a one-page concept note

Include the working title, problem, main question, three subquestions, likely evidence, and limitations. Share it with your supervisor before investing in a full proposal. A concise concept note often reveals an unclear population or an unanswerable question faster than a long introduction.

Frequently asked questions

Should a research topic be broad to provide more sources?

Usually not. A broad topic creates too many possible methods and weakens the argument. Narrow the topic by population, setting, period, and question.

Can a topic change after the literature review?

Yes. Document why the scope changed and agree the revised direction with your supervisor before collecting data.
